## Why this matters
How can whiteness be both exalted and terrifying? Ishmael's long
“though … yet” accumulation gives associations the narrator treats as
elevating weight before
turning toward panic; his final questions do not supply a simple symbol key.
A first reader might assign whiteness one meaning, but the chapter makes the
same quality carry incompatible associations.

## Two readings grounded in the text
1. **Cultural signs clash:** whiteness is associated with royal power,
   innocence and sacredness, but also shrouds and frightening animals.
   The sequence makes meaning depend on context rather than a fixed code.
   Yet Ishmael also searches for a terror that exceeds learned associations.
2. **Perception itself becomes unsettling:** the closing questions call white
   both absence and the combination of colors and imagine a landscape as
   blank yet meaningful. The contradictory language makes certainty difficult.
   Yet Ishmael's vivid examples do not permit reducing the chapter to an
   abstract optical puzzle alone.

## Educational experience
- **30 seconds:** show two short examples, one reverential and one frightening;
  ask how each changes the force of the same word. Flag that the chapter also
  contains racist and hostile language, with an optional contextualized route
  to it. Open the full paragraph and chapter rather than offering a color key.
- **Alternative:** reveal the repeated “though” clauses before the “yet,”
  letting the sentence's turn do the work.
- **3 minutes:** trace the shift from cultural symbols to Ishmael's question
  about absence and all colors; include a clearly contextualized examination
